Let’s pressure-test this directly. The impulse to protect open-source work from predatory enclosure and hold corporate giants accountable is a driving force behind copyleft and open-source movements. However, trying to change human behavior by embedding moral laws or legal restrictions directly into a programming language runs into major historical, legal, and structural roadblocks.

The Practical Flaws & Attack Vectors

The "JSON / Douglas Crockford" Precedent (Moral Clauses Don't Work):

The Concept: Douglas Crockford famously added a moral clause to the JSON license: "The Software shall be used for Good, not Evil."

The Reality: It created legal chaos. Enterprise legal teams banned JSON, and the Open Source Initiative (OSI) and Free Software Foundation (FSF) ruled it non-free / non-open-source because "Good" and "Evil" are subjective. Adding clauses that target specific industries (e.g., aviation, military, fossil fuels) turns your tool into a proprietary source-available license rather than an open standard.

The Enforcement & Litigation Trap:

The Premise: "If an airline uses our kernel code without attribution, the community can sue them."

The Reality: Lawsuits require millions of pounds, legal jurisdiction, and corporate standing. Massive corporations frequently absorb legal fines or simply rewrite the small kernel in C++ or Rust to bypass the license entirely. Copyleft works because of strict copyright law (like the GPL enforcing source disclosure), not subjective moral conditions.

Adoption Friction (The Network Effect Barrier):

If a new programming language carries legal minefields, developers and engineers simply will not adopt it. They will stick to Apache 2.0 / MIT languages (like Mojo, Rust, Python) where they don't risk a lawsuit for building standard applications.
